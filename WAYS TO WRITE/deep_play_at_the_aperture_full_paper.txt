Deep Play at the Aperture
Large Language Games and the Operative Humanities
Watson Hartsoe
September 7, 2026
Abstract
Generative interfaces have compressed historically distinct practices of language into a single
textual aperture. A query, command, score, program, probe, edit, commission, and conversation
may now enter the same box and receive an answer in the same typographic form, even though
each practice carries different conditions of authority, evidence, success, failure, and responsibility.
This paper argues that the resulting category error is not merely terminological. It produces
technical and institutional failures because systems are asked to infer the operative force of
language while interfaces hide the surrounding game. Drawing on Wittgenstein’s language-games,
Weber’s ideal type, Austin’s speech acts, Geertz’s thick description and deep play, and a practicebased corpus of generative systems, I propose an operative humanities: a mode of inquiry that
reconstructs the rules, roles, state, provenance, constraints, and consequences that make an
utterance operative. Six built cases - LDraw-based construction tests, persistent narrative state,
strategic multi-agent dialogue, controlled cinematic prompting, agent-based historical modeling,
and legible procedural film - show that the decisive design move is repeatedly the same: insert
inspectable machinery into the gap between words and convincing results. The paper concludes
that the most useful unit of analysis is not the prompt string but the language game in motion.

Introduction: One Rectangle, Twelve Institutions
A language model is given a sentence: “Draw a circle.” Nothing in those three words tells us what
sort of act has occurred. A design client may be commissioning an image. A programmer may be
testing whether a graphics agent can execute a command. A researcher may be probing geometric
competence. An artist may be supplying a score whose realization is intentionally open. A teacher
may be issuing an instruction. A performer may be making a timed move before an audience. A
multimodal user may be using the sentence together with a gesture that identifies where the circle
should go. The surface string is stable while the institution around it changes.
Contemporary generative interfaces conceal this instability by giving all of these acts the same
aperture: a rectangular text box, a send button, a transcript. The interface implies that “prompt”
names a coherent natural kind. It does not. The word instead covers a set of historically different
language practices whose differences become technically consequential once language can trigger
computation.
Wittgenstein’s language-games offer the first corrective. His builders’ game makes meaning depend
on organized activity: “slab” works because a builder and assistant are engaged in a practice in which
the call has a learned consequence. (Wittgenstein 1953) Section 23 of Philosophical Investigations
then multiplies the scene, listing diverse activities - commanding, reporting, hypothesizing, joking,
translating, asking, thanking - rather than searching for one essence of language. The methodological
1

lesson is not that all uses are incomparable. It is that an utterance becomes intelligible through the
game in which it is a move.
The present argument applies that lesson to generative systems. The prompt is best treated not as
a stable linguistic object but as a visible trace of an operative arrangement. Max Weber’s ideal type
is useful precisely because it is deliberately one-sided: it sharpens one dimension of a phenomenon
into an analytic benchmark without claiming that empirical cases instantiate it purely. (Weber
1949) Twelve ideal types - instruction, score, program, plan, query, probe, gesture, commission,
conversation, edit, constraint, performance - can therefore be used not as a taxonomy of prompts
but as a set of diagnostic exaggerations. Their value lies in revealing when the same interface hosts
incompatible criteria of success.
Austin supplies a second correction. Utterances have force, not only content. A command can
misfire; a declaration depends on felicity conditions; an apparent instruction may lack authority.
(Austin 1962) When generative interfaces collapse control text, user text, quoted material, retrieved
documents, and external content into a shared token stream, questions of force and authority become
architectural. The model cannot safely infer from imperative grammar alone which language is
entitled to reorganize the system.
Geertz supplies a third correction. Thick description asks the analyst to distinguish the wink from
the twitch by recovering the structures of interpretation that make similar movements different
acts. (Geertz 1973) “Deep play,” in his account of the Balinese cockfight, names a situation in
which the significance of the event cannot be reduced to instrumental payoff because status, rivalry,
identification, and social interpretation are being staged through it. (Geertz 1972) Generative
interaction is increasingly deep in this sense. A prompt may appear to request a paragraph or
image while also distributing authority, assigning authorship, testing trust, reorganizing memory, or
creating a record that later bears legal or institutional weight.
This paper argues that these stakes require an operative humanities: a practice of humanistic
inquiry that follows language into the systems that make it consequential. The adjective “operative”
does not mean that humanities scholarship should imitate engineering. It means that interpretation
must now include execution conditions. If a sentence can change a database, produce an image,
steer a robot, create a legal exposure, update a world state, or shape a user’s next decision, then
the meaning of the sentence includes the apparatus through which the consequence occurs.
The argument is grounded in a practice-based corpus of systems assembled in the elsewhere research
portfolio. The portfolio describes a common aim: making opaque computational processes tangible,
testable, and accountable by building the working system and studying where it fails. (Hartsoe
2026d) The cases include a LEGO construction test built on LDraw, the LEGOS narrative-state
framework, the Centaur Box multi-agent conversational architecture, CinePrompt and related
generative-film methods, Growing Entanglements and its agent-based historical models, and The
Machinery of Meaning, a procedural film engine that exposes its own operations. These systems are
not evidence that the twelve ideal types are exhaustive. They are useful because they repeatedly
force the same question: what must be made explicit before a convincing linguistic result can count
as a valid operation?

1. The Aperture Error
The design success of the chat box is also its theoretical danger. It offers a universal surface for
heterogeneous action. The same control accepts “find,” “write,” “change,” “continue,” “explain,”
2

“pretend,” “never,” “remember,” “make,” and “why.” This economy encourages a grammatical
fallacy: because all inputs are prompts, prompting must be one activity.
The error can be stated more formally. Let an utterance be represented as a string u. A promptcentric account assumes that the system’s task is to infer an appropriate response from u plus
context c:
R = f (u, c).
But the decisive variable is often not additional context in the ordinary sense. It is the operative
game g that assigns roles, authority, valid moves, evidentiary standards, state transitions, and
success conditions:
R = f (u, c, g).
The same u under different g can demand different system architectures. If g is Query, external
claims should be traceable to evidence. If g is Edit, non-target material should be conserved. If g
is Constraint, invalid outputs should be unreachable or detected. If g is Conversation, sequential
uptake and repair matter. If g is Commission, provenance and responsibility matter. If g is Probe,
controlled variation and inference limits matter. The prompt box hides g while the system must
somehow behave as if it knows it.
This is more than a UX complaint. It explains recurring failures that appear unrelated when
described at the level of model output. Prompt injection is an Instruction/Program failure because
data acquires the appearance of control. Hallucinated citations are Query failures because generation
impersonates retrieval. Silent rewriting is an Edit failure because regeneration violates conservation.
Long-horizon narrative drift is a Program/Edit failure because state is not independently maintained.
Overly rigid natural-language prompting is a Score failure because interpretive latitude is mistaken
for error. Anthropomorphic overreading is a Probe/Conversation failure because elicited discourse
is treated as evidence of interiority.
The useful engineering response is not a larger system prompt that tells the model to be careful about
all of these at once. The response is to externalize the conditions the game requires. Provenance
tags, validators, retrieval traces, explicit state stores, constrained decoders, diff viewers, planner logs,
and capability boundaries are examples of such externalization. They move part of the language
game from model interpretation into inspectable structure.
The humanistic response is equally concrete. Instead of asking only what the prompt “means,”
reconstruct the practical scene that gives it force. Who is speaking? Under what authority? To
what artifact or world state? What would count as success? What evidence could defeat the answer?
What prior commitments must survive? What part of the act is visible in the transcript and what
part occurs in hidden machinery? These are questions of interpretation, but they are interpretation
under operational pressure.

2. Deep Play in the Build: LEGO and the Refusal of Rhetoric
The LDraw experiment provides the clearest instance because plastic bricks do not negotiate with
fluent prose. Can a Language Model Build with LEGO? begins from a deliberately humiliating
3

distinction: “A picture is cheap.” A generated image can look like a LEGO truck without specifying
legal parts, exact placements, orientations, connections, or an assemblable sequence. (Hartsoe
2026b)
LDraw makes the claim falsifiable by representing construction as text. A line can name a part, color,
coordinates, and a transformation matrix. This appears to vindicate the idea that language can
become program. But the experiment immediately introduces a hierarchy of validation. Syntactic
validity only proves that the system wrote LDraw. A legal file may still describe collision, unsupported
geometry, disconnected parts, or an assembly that cannot be physically completed.
This is the difference between instruction, program, query, and constraint condensed into one toy.
“Build a red truck” is an instruction only if a legitimate execution relation exists. The LDraw file
is program-like because its text denotes explicit transformations and objects. The question “is it
buildable?” is a query because the answer must be accountable to something outside rhetorical
fluency. Buildability is a constraint because some states should be excluded rather than merely
discouraged.
The case exposes a recurrent habit in generative AI evaluation: success is often measured at the level
where the model is strongest and the world is weakest. If the benchmark scores visual resemblance,
the model can win by looking right. If the task is physical assembly, a different evidentiary regime
is required. The work’s importance therefore lies less in LEGO than in the epistemology of external
resistance. A useful generative system needs a place where its rhetoric stops.
Constraint theory makes this technically precise. Montanari’s classic account treats constraints as
relations capable of reducing a search space. (Montanari 1974) A hard constraint changes reachability.
Natural-language prompting, by contrast, usually changes probability. “Do not place unsupported
bricks” may reduce failures without making unsupported states impossible. The distinction between
“should not” and “cannot” is therefore an architectural property, not a linguistic nuance.
The humanities question enters at the boundary of formalization. Which constraints define building?
Gravity, collision, catalog legality, hand accessibility, aesthetic convention, structural strength, or
the culturally learned sense of what counts as a LEGO technique? The more serious the benchmark
becomes, the more it discovers that “buildable” is not one property but a practice. The operative
humanities does not weaken the test by saying everything is interpretive. It strengthens the test by
identifying which interpretation has been operationalized.

3. The Blueberry and the Problem of Consequence
If the brick supplies material resistance, the blueberry supplies temporal resistance. Coaxing the
Ripples begins with a bird eating a berry that later returns untouched. (Marttila, Bolter, and
Hartsoe 2026) The error is small enough to be comic and large enough to diagnose a fundamental
problem: generation can produce an event without preserving what the event changed.
The ordinary response is to call this memory failure. That is too weak. A system may retrieve the
earlier sentence perfectly and still fail to know which propositions remain active, which relations
changed, and which branch of a narrative they belong to. Memory retrieves traces. State governs
consequence.
The Ripples grammar - entity, vector, ripple - treats the world as a field of relations rather than a
stream of independent sentences. After the Scene and LEGOS extend the same wager by moving

4

narrative state outside prose: people, places, desires, obstacles, changes, and resolutions become
explicit handles that can be traced, forked, and restored. (Hartsoe 2026a)
This is where Naur’s theory-building account of programming becomes unexpectedly humanistic. A
program is not merely a text whose syntax a machine can execute. It embodies a theory of how the
domain works. (Naur 1985) A narrative-state engine similarly embodies a theory of what counts as
a consequential fact. If a key is destroyed, the system must represent the destruction as something
later generation is not free to erase casually. The model’s prose is no longer sovereign over the world
it describes.
Such systems necessarily make cuts. They decide that this entity, relation, commitment, or causal
fact deserves explicit persistence while another remains implicit in language. The model of the
world is therefore also an interpretation of the world. This is precisely why humanities expertise
matters. The question is not whether state should be formalized, but how the formalization changes
what can be remembered, contradicted, or made consequential.
The operative humanities treats that cut as evidence. What did the designer decide to store? Which
changes require explicit transitions? What kinds of ambiguity are permitted? Which contradictions
are flagged rather than silently repaired? The model architecture becomes a readable theory of
significance.

4. Strategy Before Speech: Conversation as Hidden Planning
The transcript has another way of lying by omission. It shows the utterance that occurred and
hides the options that did not.
Centaur Box makes the hidden field explicit. A conversational turn is processed through memo,
observer model, planner, selector, draft, send, and adjudication. Multiple candidate acts can be
generated before one appears as the public reply. (Hartsoe 2026c) The line in the transcript is
therefore a residue of planning.
Classical planning research formalized the relation among current state, goal state, and operators.
STRIPS is canonical because it attempts to find a sequence of operators that transforms an initial
world model into one satisfying a goal formula. (Fikes and Nilsson 1971) Suchman’s critique is
canonical because it shows how badly plan-centered accounts can misdescribe situated action when
the world’s contingencies are treated as noise rather than constitutive resources. (Suchman 1987)
Centaur Box recreates the argument at the scale of dialogue. The system can plan, but each public
reply changes the next situation. The gatekeeper’s refusal is not an error in execution; it is new
evidence. The planner must therefore remain subordinate to interaction.
Conversation analysis supplies the social machinery. Turn-taking is locally managed and sensitive
to recipient design; a later turn displays an interpretation of an earlier one. (Sacks, Schegloff,
and Jefferson 1974) Centaur Box adds a computational layer: each role responds not to the other
directly but to a current model of the other. This means that a conversation can recursively stabilize
a mistaken representation. A model that infers hostility may produce guarded moves that elicit
guarded replies and then treat those replies as confirmation.
The technical imperative is to expose the model-update loop. What evidence changed the user
model? Which candidate acts were rejected? Why was one selected? When did the planner revise

5

the goal rather than merely rephrase the strategy? The humanistic imperative is to read these
structures as part of the interaction rather than as neutral implementation detail.
The road not taken is part of the transcript because it defines the significance of the road that was
taken.

5. Thick Prompting as Field Method
The twelve ideal types would be weak if they only renamed familiar interface behaviors. Their
methodological value appears when they support controlled comparison.
The surrounding dissertation work defines thick prompting as iterative, comparative prompting
under controlled variation, with logging, comparison tables, interpretive criteria, and explicit limits.
(Hartsoe 2026f) This is a narrower and more defensible claim than the broad proposition that
all prompting is world-building. The distinction matters: the twelve games can function as an
analytic instrument without becoming the dissertation’s entire ontology. The archived research
notes explicitly defer WorldText and broad taxonomy-building from the core proposal architecture,
keeping operative description and thick prompting as the method spine. (Hartsoe 2026f)
Thick prompting can be understood as an ethnographic adaptation to black-box generative systems.
It does not produce “thick” evidence by writing longer prompts. It produces thickness by varying
conditions and preserving the traces required to interpret differences.
A minimal thick-prompting sequence has five elements:
1. a bounded question about system behavior;
2. a controlled family of prompt variations;
3. preserved outputs and execution conditions;
4. an explicit criterion for what counts as a meaningful difference;
5. a statement of what the sequence cannot establish.
This resembles experimental probing more than prompt optimization. Turing’s imitation game
replaced an essence question with an operational situation. (Turing 1950) Hacking’s philosophy of
experiment emphasizes intervention as a way of learning what representation alone cannot settle.
(Hacking 1983) Adversarial examples show how carefully designed perturbations reveal boundaries
invisible under ordinary inputs. (Goodfellow, Shlens, and Szegedy 2014)
But generative prompting adds a reflexive complication: the linguistic probe can scaffold the very
behavior it purports to measure. A question about machine fear may create a local discourse
in which fearful first-person language is the most plausible continuation. The analyst therefore
needs matched probes, negative controls, contrary framings, and session resets. The point is not to
eliminate interpretation but to make the inference proportional to the trace.
Growing Entanglements offers a parallel method at the scale of historical simulation. Seven agentbased models are not treated as seven answers to Captain Cook’s death. Each is an apparatus that
isolates a mechanism, and the difference among runs becomes data about what each ontology can
see. (Hartsoe 2026e) Changing a parameter tests sensitivity; changing the ontology tests the cut
through which the historical field is being represented.
This is an operative version of Geertz’s thick description. The analyst does not merely add context
around a computational result. The analyst reconstructs the structures that made that result
available: model, prompt, state, selection rule, evidence source, and failure criterion.

6. From Imagetext to Operative Force
The broader theoretical trajectory of the work begins in ekphrasis: language addressed to visual
production. Operative Ekphrasis, written with Jay David Bolter, treats description as a control
surface that participates in producing what it names. (Hartsoe and Bolter 2026) The important
shift is from representation to operation.
This claim becomes stronger when it is not universalized. Not every description becomes operative
merely because it is sent to a model. Operativity is achieved through an arrangement: model,
interface, permissions, generative medium, state, constraints, and human uptake. The prompt is
one component in a causal chain.
The distinction answers a common objection from technical readers. “Prompts are just inputs”
is true at one level and inadequate at another. A shell command is also an input; so is a SQL
query, a legal filing, a theatrical cue, and a medical order. The analytic question is what institution
interprets the input and what transitions the institution permits it to trigger.
It also answers a common risk in humanities writing about AI. If “language becomes executable”
is treated as a metaphor, the argument can float free of engineering detail. The practice-based
cases prevent that escape. LDraw specifies parts and transformations. LEGOS externalizes state.
Centaur Box logs candidate moves and updates profiles. CinePrompt distinguishes invariants from
variables. Growing Entanglements instantiates explicit agent rules. Machinery of Meaning exposes
its own procedural sequence. Operative force is not an atmosphere around the prompt. It is built.

7. Show Us Your Screens: Performance and Legibility
The Machinery of Meaning supplies the final case because it makes inspection itself aesthetic. The
portfolio calls it a procedural film engine that can be read while it runs. (Hartsoe 2026g) Its
code stages question, example, source proposition, diagram, and citation as timed elements. The
procedure is part of the viewing experience.
Live coding offers a useful lineage. TOPLAP’s manifesto demands that screens be visible and
algorithms exposed alongside their outcomes. (TOPLAP 2004) Prompt Battle similarly turns
text-to-image control into public competition in which timing, failure, and recovery become part of
what an audience judges. (Prompt Battle 2022)
This matters methodologically because generative artifacts erase their own production history. A
compelling image can hide twenty failed attempts; a fluent answer can hide retrieval failures and
rejected drafts; a successful agent action can hide dangerous candidate plans. If evaluation begins
at the final output, the architecture has already disappeared.
The operative humanities therefore treats legibility as a design value. Logs, diffs, source maps, state
views, rejected candidates, constraint violations, and provenance markers are not merely debugging
conveniences. They are interpretive surfaces. They allow the human participant to reconstruct why
the system acted as it did and which alternatives remained possible.
Legibility does not mean total transparency. A neural model cannot be made fully inspectable by
adding a dashboard. Nor does more telemetry automatically produce understanding. Goodwin’s
account of professional vision reminds us that seeing is organized by practices of highlighting and
coding. (Goodwin 1994) The interface decides what counts as a visible feature. The goal is therefore

7

not maximal exposure but accountable selection: show the structures that materially govern the
language game.

8. The Operative Humanities
The six cases point toward a common research program. In each, a generative system becomes more
interpretable when some part of the language game is externalized:
• LEGO externalizes validity into a formal representation and physical checks.
• LEGOS externalizes consequence into persistent world state.
• Centaur Box externalizes strategy into candidate acts, profiles, and selection traces.
• CinePrompt externalizes interpretation into invariants, variables, and refusal criteria.
• Growing Entanglements externalizes modeling assumptions into explicit mechanisms.
• Machinery of Meaning externalizes procedure into the visible temporal surface.
The shared move is not “add structure.” It is make the conditions of operative force available
for inspection and refusal.
This is where the term operative humanities earns its place. Traditional humanistic interpretation
is often caricatured by engineers as post hoc commentary on artifacts after the real system has
already been built. The cases here suggest a different division of labor. Humanistic distinctions can
become architectural requirements.
If Austin teaches that force depends on felicity conditions, provenance and authority need explicit
representation. If conversation analysis teaches that meaning is sequential, turn history and repair
should not be flattened into isolated messages. If textual scholarship teaches that revision is a
history of differences, editing interfaces should show semantic deltas. If anthropology teaches
that a wink differs from a twitch through social context, multimodal systems should preserve the
referential field rather than only extracted labels. If art sociology teaches that making is distributed,
generative provenance should track delegation rather than oscillate opportunistically between “tool”
and “agent.” (Becker 1982; Gell 1998)
Technical thinkers cannot dismiss these as “soft” concerns without changing the task. The questions
determine what the system must store, validate, expose, and refuse. Humanities concepts become
design constraints because language has entered execution pipelines.
Humanists cannot remain satisfied with critique at the level of discourse either. Once a claim is
operational, the critic must be willing to follow it into schemas, matrices, event loops, state machines,
retrieval indexes, and model traces. Otherwise the most consequential interpretive decisions remain
hidden under implementation detail.
The operative humanities is therefore neither engineering with literary citations nor criticism
with screenshots. It is the study and construction of systems in which cultural distinctions have
computational consequences.

9. Limitations and Open Territory
The twelve language games are ideal types, not empirical species. Real sessions frequently contain
several at once. A request to “shorten this paragraph and tell me what your edit reveals about the
argument” combines Edit, Probe, and Conversation. A cinematic prompt may be Score, Commission,
8

and Performance depending on how it is used. The framework should therefore be evaluated by
whether it reveals consequential differences, not whether annotators can force every utterance into
one box.
The current evidence is also practice-based and selective. The elsewhere corpus was built to
investigate particular problems in generative media, not sampled to represent all prompt use.
Several language games are richly instantiated - Probe, Constraint, Program, Edit - while others,
especially Gesture and Query in their pure forms, are thinner. This asymmetry is itself evidence
about the research practice but limits generalization.
The archival dissertation note introduces a productive contradiction. It narrows the formal research
contribution to operative description and thick prompting, explicitly deferring broader WorldText
and taxonomy claims. (Hartsoe 2026f) The present paper should therefore be read as a working
theoretical extension, not as evidence that the dissertation has already adopted a twelve-part
architecture.
There are also technical limits to “making the game explicit.” Semantic constraints, social authority,
authorship, relevance, and conversational reciprocity cannot be perfectly encoded. The framework
should not become a fantasy that every cultural ambiguity can be converted into a schema. Residual
human theory remains necessary precisely because the system’s explicit model is always a cut
through a larger practice.
Finally, the operative humanities must avoid a new transparency ideology. More logs can create
new forms of opacity. A hundred telemetry panels may make a system less interpretable than one
carefully chosen state transition. The measure is not volume of disclosure but whether a person can
reconstruct consequential action and identify where inference exceeds evidence.

Conclusion: The Game in Motion
The prompt became important because language acquired new consequences. But “prompt” is
too coarse a noun for the work those consequences now perform. A sentence can instruct, score,
program, plan, query, probe, point, commission, converse, edit, constrain, or perform. Each game
carries a different account of authority, evidence, temporality, error, and responsibility.
The single chat aperture hides these differences. That hiding is productive when it lets people move
fluidly across tasks. It is dangerous when the system’s architectural guarantees do not move with
them.
The most consequential lesson from the built cases is therefore not that prompts need better wording.
It is that language games need infrastructure. A world needs state. A query needs evidence. An
edit needs a delta. A constraint needs enforcement. A conversation needs sequence. A commission
needs provenance. A probe needs controls. An instruction needs authority. A performance needs an
event trace.
Geertz wrote that societies contain their own interpretations. Generative systems increasingly
contain executable interpretations: encoded assumptions about what an object is, which relation
matters, what can change, what must persist, and whose words may act. The humanities can either
describe those interpretations after the fact or help make them inspectable while they run.
The operative humanities chooses the latter. Its fundamental unit is not the prompt string. It is
the language game in motion: words, roles, rules, state, apparatus, consequence, and the human
9

capacity to say that the game has changed.
